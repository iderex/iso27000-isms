# Awareness training for all staff

The course material for the awareness training. The language-neutral data stands
in the `meta.yaml` beside it, the question set in `en.gift`. No link points at a
GIFT file, because format rule 4 settles links on `.md`. The German version
stands in [de.md](de.md).

## 1. What this training presupposes

Nothing. Anyone starting here needs no prior knowledge of information security,
no standard and no step of the learning path.

Presupposed is only that somebody works in an organisation and handles
information there that belongs to others: customer data, data about colleagues,
accounts, devices.

## 2. What this training leaves out

The standards are left out. No clause number and no standard wording stands
here. Which requirement stands behind this training stands in the `meta.yaml`
beside it and not in the material, because a clause number is ballast for
somebody who does not need it.

The management system is left out. How an ISMS is built, who cuts the scope and
what stands in a statement of applicability belongs in the specialist trainings.

The tools of your own organisation are left out. Which program is used for
reports and what the access administration is called is not known to this
repository. Where the material names a place, it names a role, and on use that
role is replaced by the name it has in the house.

This training is not a shortened specialist training. It is a topic of its own,
because the question is a different one: not how security gets built, but what
one's own behaviour contributes to it.

## 3. The material

Six situations. They are built along actions and not along clauses.

### 3.1 A message that pushes for action

How to recognise it: it wants something immediately, it creates pressure or
curiosity, and it leads to a login, a payment or a file.

What to do: do not click the link in the message; call the place up the usual
way instead. In doubt, phone the sender on a number that does not stand in the
message.

What is not bad: reporting a message that turns out to be genuine. What is bad:
not reporting one that was not.

### 3.2 An account that belongs to one person

Every password and every second factor belongs to exactly one person. Anyone
passing their account on has also signed for everything done with it afterwards.

What to do: a separate password for every service, kept in the program the
organisation provides for it. Never say the second factor out loud, not on the
phone either, not to your own IT department either.

Where it pinches in daily work: a stand-in needs access. That is a question for
the access administration and not a reason to share a password.

### 3.3 A device outside the building

A laptop on a train, a phone in a café, papers in a car. Loss is likelier here
than any attack.

What to do: lock the screen as soon as you get up. Do not leave paper lying
about. Report a loss immediately, at the weekend too, even when the device will
probably turn up again.

Why immediately: the time between loss and report is the time in which nobody
can do anything.

### 3.4 Data being passed on

Before every send, the question: does the recipient need this, and do they need
it in full. An extract is often enough.

What to do: check the recipients before sending, especially with names the
program suggests. With several recipients, check whether they may know about
each other.

What does not go into a private account and not onto a private storage device:
anything belonging to the organisation or to its customers.

### 3.5 A mistake that has already happened

The wrong file sent, the link clicked, the password entered. After that one
thing counts: that somebody hears about it.

What to do: report it, immediately, to the place the organisation has named for
it. Delete nothing and cover nothing up, because the traces are needed.

What the organisation owes for that: that a report brings no punishment with it.
Where it does, nothing gets reported any more, and then the beginning of every
response is missing.

### 3.6 A change or a departure

Anyone changing roles or leaving takes accounts along that nobody needs any
more. That is the state in which somebody later comes in on an old account.

What to do: on a change, say yourself what you no longer need. Hand back devices
and keys and have that confirmed.

## 4. A worked place

Invented, like everything in this example.

A caseworker gets a message on Friday afternoon, supposedly from the management:
an invoice has to be paid today, the papers are behind the attached link, please
no questions by phone, they are in a meeting.

This is how it runs through:

1. Recognise the three marks: haste, an unusual route, questions ruled out. Each
   on its own is harmless, all three together are the pattern.
2. Do not click. The link is not opened, not "just to look" either.
3. Check the usual way. Phone the management on the known number, not on one
   from the message.
4. Report it, whatever the call turns up. The report goes to the place named in
   the house, with the message attached.
5. If it has already been clicked: report all the same, and first. Do not switch
   the device off, change the password on another device.

Result: the case is either settled in ten minutes or it is the beginning of a
response that would otherwise have started days later.

## 5. Where the wording stands

This training refers to no clause, so no licensed copy is needed to use it.

Which requirements stand behind the training stands in the `meta.yaml` beside
it, by standard, clause and edition. Those numbers were checked against several
public secondary sources that agree on them, on 2026-08-06, and not against a
licensed copy.

No licensed copy was looked into for this training.

## 6. What this training does not evidence

The evidence of what somebody learned arises in the importing system and not
here. A question set becomes a test there, the test produces attempts, points and
a pass mark, and those stand in the course report of the importing system. This
repository supplies material, questions and model answers and carries no evidence
about any one person.
