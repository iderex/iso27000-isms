## 2. What it is about

This part deals with methods where the public part of the key arises from an
identifier, for instance from an address.

The first point is the gain. Anyone wanting to write to a person needs no key
from them and no certificate about it. The identifier is enough. With that the
question of whom a public key belongs to falls away, and in operation that is the
most tiresome question there is.

The second point is the price, and it is the construction rather than a weakness.
The private parts get generated by a central body. That body can therefore read
everything encrypted for the participants. Anyone reading this chapter for one
sentence only reads that one.

The third point follows from it. Whether such a body should stand in the house is
a decision about people and not about technology. In a house with patient data it
reads: there is a place that can open every message, and who works there matters
more than any method.

The fourth point is revocation. You do not change an identifier the way you
change a key. If a private part has reached the wrong hands, revoking a
certificate does not help, because there is none. What happens then belongs
settled before the introduction and not after it.

The fifth point is availability. If the central body fails, nobody can obtain a
new private part. Encrypting goes on; decrypting for new participants does not.

What does not stand here is the wording. Anyone who needs it looks it up in a
licensed copy.

## 3. For whom, and for whom not

For anyone designing an exchange where managing public keys is too laborious.

For anyone who has to decide whether a body with this capability should stand in
the house.

For anyone reading an offer in which this construction appears who wants to know
which question it does not answer.

Not for anyone wanting to do without such a central body. That is
[part 2](../iso-iec-18033-2/en.md).

Not for anyone wanting to agree a key. That is
[ISO/IEC 11770-3](../iso-iec-11770-3/en.md).

Not as a way of saving the management of public keys without paying the price.
The price stands in section 2.

## 5. What you do with it

You first write down who works at the central body and what those people can do.
Without that sentence no further question is meaningful.

Then you settle the issuing of identifiers. Whoever gets an identifier gets
everything sent to it.

Then you settle revocation: what happens when a private part is lost or a person
leaves the house.

Then you settle the failure of the central body and the storage of its own
secret.

Then you write down what this construction saves against the alternative and what
it costs, and you put both side by side.

In operation what remains is oversight of the central body. It is the place where
everything comes together.

## 6. Boundary against the neighbouring standard

Against [part 2](../iso-iec-18033-2/en.md): there the private part belongs to
whoever generated it. Here it comes from outside, and that is the whole
difference.

Against [ISO/IEC 11770-3](../iso-iec-11770-3/en.md): there two sides agree. Here
a third issues.

Against [ISO/IEC 29191](../iso-iec-29191/en.md): there the subject is not being
named. Here the identifier is the key, so the opposite.

Against [ISO/IEC 27565](../iso-iec-27565/en.md): there a property gets proved
without handing over the detail. Both touch the question of how much an
identifier gives away.

Against a public key infrastructure: it solves the same task at a different
price, and the comparison belongs in the decision.

## 7. Precondition and what follows

Presupposed is a decision on whether a body with this capability may exist.

Presupposed is an orderly issuing of identifiers.

Presupposed is a place for the central body's secret that withstands a failure.

What follows is oversight of that body and the rule for revocation.

## 8. Walk-through: writing the price down before the gain

This walk-through follows the pattern in
[tutorials/en.md](../../tutorials/en.md). The example is invented.

Assume a hospital group where clinicians from different houses are to write to
each other encrypted. Managing public keys across three houses has failed on
effort. An offer proposes this construction. The question is: what gets traded?

Step 1, write down the gain. Nobody has to look up or check a key any more. The
address is enough.

Step 2, write down the price, in the same paragraph. A body arises that can open
every message between all participants.

Step 3, staff the body. Who works there, who oversees it, and in which of the
three houses does it stand. In a group that question is harder than in one house,
and it is the actual subject.

Step 4, think revocation through. A notebook gets lost. The address stays the
same. What does revocation mean here, and who learns of it.

Step 5, think failure through. If the central body stands still, new participants
can no longer read.

Step 6, put the alternative beside it. What would managing public keys really
have cost, and what did it fail on.

Step 7, take the boundary into the register. The capability of the central body
goes as a line into the risk register following
[templates/registers/risk-register/en.md](../../templates/registers/risk-register/en.md),
with what an abuse there would mean for the people concerned.

What comes out of it: gain and price side by side, a staffed and overseen body,
an answer on revocation, one on failure, a comparison with the alternative and a
line in the register. What does not come out of it: a recommendation for or
against this construction. This chapter gives none.

The assumptions of this example: three houses, one group, one offer. Anyone
staying inside a single house answers step 3 more easily and keeps the rest.

## 12. State

This chapter refers to ISO/IEC 18033-5:2015 as the edition in force. The catalog
entry for it carries `confirmation: confirmed`, resting on two independent
sources, and was read on 2026-08-04. It carries one amendment, `amd-1:2021`,
whose content is not read and not judged here.

The clause and control numbers in sections 4 and 11 are checked against the tree
and not against a licensed copy. They come from the tables that sit in the tree
and carry their own reading date; the commands stand in the German half.

No clause number from ISO/IEC 18033-5 itself gets named, and that is deliberate.
A number nobody has looked up is worse than none: it looks checkable.

No name of a method and no key length stands in this chapter. The standard
carries such names, and reproducing them would be an adopted list; the boundary
in `copyright/en.md` rules that out.

That a central body generates the private parts and can therefore read along, and
that an identifier cannot be changed the way a key can, are general properties of
this construction and not taken from this standard. Whether a particular
implementation softens that property has not been examined here.

The hospital group in the example is invented. No statement follows from it about
how a group should manage its keys.

This edition is from 2015. Whether a particular method carried in it is fit for a
particular purpose today has not been judged here, and none gets recommended.
This construction is neither advised for nor against.
