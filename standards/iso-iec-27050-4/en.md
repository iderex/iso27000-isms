## 2. What it is about

This part deals with what a system has to be able to do for a production to be
possible and affordable at all.

The first point is when it gets bought. This capability is bought when a system
is chosen and not at the moment it is needed. A system that cannot be searched
by person and by period and that can hand nothing over in full will not manage
it under a deadline either. The price of that property is low at selection and
close to unpayable afterwards.

The second point is that an export nobody has tried is an assumption. Almost
every system has a button for it. Whether the result is complete, whether the
entries about origin travel with it, whether a message keeps its attachments and
how long a run over two years takes is known only to somebody who has done it
once.

The third point is the stores nobody counts as a system. A department's
messaging service, a shared drive, a ticket system, the voicemail of a number, a
device in somebody's bag. The largest gaps sit there and not in the big
applications, because somebody thought about the big ones.

The fourth point is the pull in the opposite direction. Deleting is right, and
the less there is, the less there is to hand over. Readiness does not mean
keeping everything but knowing what is kept and for how long. A written
retention rule serves both sides; its absence serves neither.

The fifth point is the proof. There is exactly one worth anything: a complete
export for one person and one month, with a measured time beside it. That single
figure replaces every description of what the system supposedly does.

What does not stand here is the wording. Whoever needs it opens a licensed copy.

## 3. Whom it serves, and whom it does not

For anyone choosing or replacing a system who wants to know which question to
put.

For anyone running a store who has never tried what comes out of it.

For anyone writing a retention rule and noticing that deleting and producing
pull in different directions.

Not for whoever has a demand in the house. That is
[part 3](../iso-iec-27050-3/en.md), and this part arrives too late for it.

Not for whoever wants to settle responsibility. That is
[part 2](../iso-iec-27050-2/en.md).

Not as a substitute for a procurement rule. What a supplier owes in general
stands in [ISO/IEC 27036-2](../iso-iec-27036-2/en.md).

## 5. What a practitioner does with it

Take the register of stores from part 1 and enter three questions per line:
searchable by person, searchable by period, exportable in full. Three ticks or
three crosses.

Then try it. One person, one month, a complete export, and beside it the
measured time. That costs a morning per store and is the only load-bearing
statement in this chapter.

Then look at what the export loses. Attachments, entries about origin, links
between messages, formatting. What is lost is, in a dispute, where the other
side starts.

Then write the questions from the first step into procurement. They cost nothing
there and cannot be had later.

Then settle the retention. One period per store, written down, with a note on
how it is stopped in the event of an order.

In running operation the date stays: every new store gets the three questions
before it goes live, not afterwards.

## 6. Where it stops against the neighbour

Against [part 3](../iso-iec-27050-3/en.md): there the work runs once the demand
has arrived. Here things are set up beforehand.

Against [part 2](../iso-iec-27050-2/en.md): there the effort is weighed. That
weighing presupposes somebody knowing what is possible, and that stands here.

Against [ISO/IEC 27040](../iso-iec-27040/en.md): there the subject is storage
and its security. Here it is whether something can be got out of a store, and
that is a question for the application and not for the storage.

Against [ISO/IEC 27555](../iso-iec-27555/en.md): there stands the deletion of
entries about people. It pulls the other way, and both rules belong in the same
retention settlement.

Against [ISO/IEC 27031](../iso-iec-27031/en.md): there the subject is readiness
for an outage. A backup restores a state; an export gives a slice to third
parties. Both produce copies and solve different jobs.

## 8. Walk-through: trying one store for production once

This walk-through follows the pattern in
[tutorials/en.md](../../tutorials/en.md). The example is invented.

Take a hospital running a department's messaging service and never having
checked what can be got out of it. The question is: what is known after one
morning that was not known before?

Step 1, choose a person and a month, with their agreement or from a test area.
Whoever uses a real history settles beforehand who may allow that.

Step 2, start the complete export and measure the time. In this example it takes
twenty minutes, and that is good news.

Step 3, look at the result. In this example the attachments are missing, the
messages stand with no relation to each other, and who read what and when is
absent entirely. That is the real finding of the morning.

Step 4, extrapolate. Six people over two years is not twenty minutes times one
hundred and forty-four, because the export is started per person and somebody
has to sit with it. That figure belongs in the bill for effort from
[part 2](../iso-iec-27050-2/en.md).

Step 5, write the three questions from section 5 into the procurement documents,
so that the next service has to answer them.

Step 6, write the boundary. In this example the store stays exportable without
attachments, and the service gives no more. That is a knowingly accepted danger
and gets a line in the risk register. The pattern stands in
[templates/registers/risk-register/en.md](../../templates/registers/risk-register/en.md).

What comes out of it: a measured time, a list of what is missing, an
extrapolation, three questions in procurement and a line in the register. What
does not come out of it: a store that can do more than before. The morning
changes nothing about it, it only says where things stand.

The assumptions of this example: a service with no tested export, a test person,
a house that cannot change the service. Whoever can change it has the real act
in step 5 and not in step 6.

## 12. As read

This chapter refers to ISO/IEC 27050-4:2021 as the edition in force. Its catalog
entry carries `confirmation: confirmed`, resting on two independent sources, and
was read on 2026-08-04. It carries no amendment and names no superseded edition.
The command and its output stand in the German half.

The clause and control numbers in sections 4 and 11 are checked against the tree
and not against a licensed copy. They come from the tables that sit in the tree
and carry a read date of their own, which the German half prints. The same
calculation over `mappings/external/cis-controls.csv` gives 47 rows and over
`mappings/external/bsi-it-grundschutz.csv` 72 rows, both with the same date. A
number appearing in none of those three tables does not stand in this chapter.

No clause number of ISO/IEC 27050-4 itself is named, and that is deliberate. A
number nobody looked up is worse than none: it looks checkable.

The requirements this part puts on a system do not stand here, neither by name
nor in number, and neither do the maturity levels it works with. Reproducing
either would be an adopted list; the boundary in `copyright/en.md` rules that
out. The three questions in section 5 are this chapter's own and are the smallest
set that tells one store from another.

That the largest gaps sit in the stores nobody counts as a system is a general
observation about grown houses and is not taken from this standard.

Not measured is how long an export takes in any particular product. The twenty
minutes and what is missing in section 8 are assumptions of the example and not
a statement about a product.

No product, no tool and no supplier is recommended here. The three questions from
section 5 are to be put to every system and are answered here for none.

No licensed copy was consulted for this chapter.

Whether a new edition has appeared since the date named is not said by this
chapter.
