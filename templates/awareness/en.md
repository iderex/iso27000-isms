# Awareness material, description and patterns

This file describes four pieces of awareness material and gives the structure of
each. It says what occasion a piece is for, how long it takes and what should be
different afterwards.

A worked example stands in [example.en.md](example.en.md). The German version is
[de.md](de.md).

## 1. What this material is for

Awareness addresses everyone who works for an organisation, and not only the
people who deal with information security. It should bring about that a person
acts correctly in the moment they make a decision, without looking anything up
first.

It is not a shortened specialist training. A specialist training conveys material
and tests it; awareness material recalls a few things and works by appearing
often and briefly. Whoever shortens a specialist training gets a poor training
and not awareness material.

The awareness training with its question set is a different thing and sits
elsewhere. Material stands here.

## 2. The link to the standard

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 asks in 7.3 for awareness. The requirement covers not only
employees but everyone working for the organisation. What the clause asks for in
detail does not stand here; anyone needing it opens 7.3 in a licensed copy.

That reference stands in this description and not in the material itself. A
poster naming a clause does not get read, and material quoting a standard
breaches the boundary at the first opportunity.

How the clause number was checked stands openly beside it: against several public
secondary sources that agree on it, and not against a licensed copy.

## 3. The four pieces

Four, because they meet different moments: a place, an inbox, a meeting and the
first working day. Whoever does only one of them does the first; whoever does all
four repeats the same message in four layers.

Placeholders stand in capitals and get replaced. What does not apply to an
organisation is answered rather than deleted.

### 3.1 The poster

- Occasion: permanent, at the place where the action happens.
- Duration: twenty seconds in passing.
- What should be different afterwards: one single action gets carried out
  correctly without anybody having to think.

Structure:

1. One line naming the situation: SITUATION.
2. One line saying what to do: ACTION.
3. One line saying whom to ask: ROLE and HOW TO REACH THEM.

A poster carries one action and not three. Whoever writes down three gets none.

### 3.2 The short message

- Occasion: monthly, or immediately after an event that concerns everyone.
- Duration: one minute of reading.
- What should be different afterwards: a person does one particular thing today,
  such as checking a device or reporting a message.

Structure:

1. A subject line naming the action rather than the topic.
2. Two to four sentences: what happened or what is coming, OCCASION.
3. One sentence with the action: ACTION, by DATE.
4. One sentence on whom to turn to: ROLE.

No attachments, no references to a document that has to be opened first. Whoever
has to open something for the action does not do it.

### 3.3 The five minutes in a meeting

- Occasion: a meeting that happens anyway.
- Duration: five minutes.
- What should be different afterwards: every person present can say whom they
  call on a suspicion.

Structure:

1. A case from the organisation itself, or an invented one that fits: CASE.
2. The question to the room: what would you have done?
3. The organisation's answer: ACTION.
4. The route: ROLE, HOW TO REACH THEM, and what happens after a report is made.

The fourth point is the most important and the most often left out. Whoever does
not know what happens after a report does not report the next time.

### 3.4 The first working day

- Occasion: a person joining, including one who only works there for a while.
- Duration: fifteen minutes in conversation.
- What should be different afterwards: the new person knows the three rules that
  apply to them and knows whom to ask.

Structure:

1. The three rules that hold for this role: RULE 1, RULE 2, RULE 3.
2. How to notice that something is wrong: SIGNS.
3. Whom to ask and whom to report to: ROLE.
4. One sentence that questions are welcome and that reporting has no consequences
   for the person reporting.

Three rules, not the whole policy. The policy sits beside it and gets read when
it is needed.

## 4. What does not belong in awareness material

No text from a standard, neither verbatim nor as a paraphrase that follows the
structure of the original.

No threat. Material working with consequences for the individual lowers the
number of reports and not the number of mistakes.

No number without a source. A statement like "ninety per cent of all incidents
begin with" gets looked up or left out.

No picture that exposes a person, not even as a joke.

No action the organisation does not make possible. Whoever calls for reporting
says where to, and makes sure somebody is there.

## 5. What this material is not

No check enforces it. Nothing runs in this repository today that refuses a piece
of awareness material for putting three actions on one poster. This description
is read by a person.

It is not evidence either. That material was put up says nothing about whether
anybody acts differently afterwards. What would be evidence comes out of the
training and its question set, and that sits elsewhere.

It is not consulting. What stands here is written generally and does not know the
situation of any one organisation.
