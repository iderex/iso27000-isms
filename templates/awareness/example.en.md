# Example: the four pieces filled in

This example fills in the four pieces from [en.md](en.md) once. It is invented.
The German version is [example.de.md](example.de.md).

## The assumptions of this example

Without these assumptions the example cannot be carried over to another
situation:

- The organisation is an invented physiotherapy group practice with twelve staff,
  the same one as in the other templates. No entry comes from a real
  organisation.
- There is a reception computer in a public area, three laptops and a patient
  records system at a software provider.
- The practice manager is the role reports go to, and is reachable during opening
  hours. Where an organisation has a reporting point of its own, its name stands
  there instead.
- There is a team meeting on Monday morning that happens anyway. Without such a
  meeting the third piece would fall away, and that would stand here.
- The rules the material refers to stand in the policy on mobile devices and
  remote access in the example under `templates/policies/`.

## 1. The poster

At reception, at eye level beside the screen:

> You are stepping away for a moment.
>
> Lock the screen, even for two minutes.
>
> Questions: the practice manager, in the office during opening hours.

One action, not three. Encryption and reporting routes do not stand on this
poster, because they are needed in another place and another moment.

## 2. The short message

Subject: please check the lock on your own device today

> Twice last week the reception computer stood unlocked while nobody was at the
> desk. Nothing happened, and that is what makes now the good moment for this.
>
> Please check today, on the device you work at, whether the screen locks itself
> after ten minutes at the latest. If it does not, tell the practice manager, who
> will pass it to the IT service provider.
>
> Questions go to the practice manager.

No attachment. Whoever would have to open a document for the action does not do
it.

## 3. The five minutes in the team meeting

The case:

> On Friday a message arrives, supposedly from the software provider. There has
> been an error in the appointment booking, please sign in briefly with your
> credentials so the appointments are not lost. The sender looks as it usually
> does, and the message arrives on Friday afternoon.

The question to the room: what would you have done?

The practice's answer:

> Click nothing, enter nothing. The provider never asks for credentials. Tell the
> practice manager, in person or on the mobile.

The route afterwards:

> The practice manager calls the provider on the number from the contract, not
> the one from the message. Afterwards she tells the team what it was. A report
> that turns out to be harmless was a correct report, and it has no consequences
> for anybody.

The last paragraph is the one most likely to be dropped, and it is the one that
decides next time whether anything gets reported.

## 4. The first working day

Fifteen minutes in conversation, led by the practice manager.

The three rules for a person at reception:

> 1. You work with your own account, not with somebody else's.
> 2. The screen is locked when you leave the desk.
> 3. Patient data does not leave the practice on your private device.

How to notice that something is wrong:

> Somebody asks for a password. A message pushes for haste. A device behaves
> differently from usual.

Whom to ask and whom to report to:

> Both the practice manager. When in doubt, once too often rather than once too
> few.

The closing sentence:

> Questions are welcome, the same one twice included. A report has no
> consequences for the person reporting, not even when it turns out the mistake
> was ours.
